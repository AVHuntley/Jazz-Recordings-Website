  - name: Nancy Wilson

  prose: |-
    Wilson was named an [NEA Jazz Master](https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/nancy-wilson) in 2004. That linked article provides a short biography.

    If you've been listening to the [NPR *Jazz Profiles*](https://www.npr.org/series/10208861/npr-s-jazz-profiles) links posted about previous artists we've studied, then you've heard Wilson's voice already. She was the host of this program from 1996 to 2005.

    Wilson was friends with Cannonball Adderley, and it was he who encouraged Wilson to move to New York to pursue music. They even recorded an [album](https://open.spotify.com/album/6yyNgCQNsJ3VJ9PP7CmvLJ?si=y8V85AkpTU-F4qjqnBQ6ww) together that is [well-remembered](https://www.npr.org/2011/06/20/4559119/nancy-wilson-nancy-wilson-cannonball-adderley) .

    Her singing style is often described as conversational. She slips in and out of strict singing and a kind of pitched storytelling.

    Wilson’s demos of “Guess Who I Saw Today” and “Sometimes I’m Happy” were what prompted Capitol Records to sign her in 1960. - heading: Shirley Horn
  artists:
  - name: Shirley Horn
    born: 1934
    died: 2005
  prose: |-
    Read the [Grove Music Online article about Shirley Horn](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2228503) .

    Shirley Horn was a pianist and vocalist, although she wasn’t recorded doing both at once for some time. She was noted for her ability to accompany herself with independence.

    Check out [this poetically-written interview with Horn from JazzTimes](https://jazztimes.com/features/profiles/shirley-horn-around-the-horn-with-shirley/) , originally published in 2001. It notes that Horn was the recipient of a rare Miles Davis endorsement (he wasn't known for giving compliments).

    The basic outline of Horn’s career has been told and retold, beginning with her “discovery” by Miles Davis in 1960.... Evidently Davis had been so taken with Horn’s first album, *Embers and Ashes* , that he refused to work at the Village Vanguard without her.

    Like so many of the jazz greats, Horn learned by carefully copying the music of an idol:

    “I just knew, ‘I got to play that piano,’ right?” she says, describing how she acquired her jazz chops. “Then I got interested in Erroll Garner and started listening to him. I knew his things note for note. And slowly I learned."

    Her first record was with jazz violinist [Stuff Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuff_Smith) , but the first editions of that album incorrectly credited another person! The interview describes Horn's style as featuring **well-placed block chords complementing mellow, spare melodic lines** .

    Horn's 1991 album [*You Won’t Forget Me*](https://open.spotify.com/album/2MdWXhbBIjWF25jftZJ0ng?si=Ux-sftznQ32tVmfVqQj9og) was a multi-week best-selling jazz album in the US. It featured Miles Davis in a rare supporting position.

    Her vocal tone has been described as "smokey."

- heading: Nina Simone
  artists:
  - name: Nina Simone
    born: 1933
    died: 2003
  prose: |-
    Look at [Nina Simone's official biography from her website](http://www.ninasimone.com/bio/) .

    Nina Simone was both a pianist and vocalist. She is remembered mostly for her singing but is underappreciated as a pianist. For example, check out [her rendition of Tadd Dameron's bebop standard "Good Bait,"](https://open.spotify.com/track/3VtdvomG4MZVbmZ1V5Ys2k?si=D9a8QCjmR_aVKGYDBgUWMw) from the same album as the selection below. It features a "classical" sensibility that sounds decades ahead of its time. (In fact, her playing reminds me a little of [Marian McPartland's]($WIKI_REFERENCE$/pages/2-dot-2-bebop-pianists) .)

    Born Eunice Kathleen Waymon, she studied classical piano at Juilliard but found it difficult to pursue a career as a concert pianist, at least in part because of her gender and race. She adopted the stage name "Nina Simone" so her family wouldn’t know she played jazz. According to her official bio:

    In order to hide the fact that she was singing in bars, Eunice’s mother would refer to the practice as “working in the fires of hell”. Overnight, Eunice Waymon became Nina Simone by taking the nickname “Nina” meaning “little one” in Spanish and “Simone” after the actress Simone Signoret.

    In the 1960s, Simone's music became overtly about the ongoing Civil Rights Movement. She wrote and performed the well-known protest song [“Mississippi Goddamn”](https://open.spotify.com/track/2UG2WPDBg6tQ8nhi1cqJRP?si=HKdaDta7R6mtVBBaHf5bSw) .

    If you have access to Netflix, check out the documentary [*What Happened, Miss Simone?*](https://www.netflix.com/title/70308063) It provides a lot of insight into both her career and chronic mental health challenges.

- heading: Mose Allison
  artists:
  - name: Mose Allison
    born: 1927
    died: 2016
  prose: |-
    There are two relevant Grove Music Online articles on Mose Allison.

    The [first](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2226996) mentions the influence of the blues:

    His style was influenced by the blues music he heard on the jukebox at his father’s general store.

    Allison created a hybrid style that integrated country blues with urbane jazz.

    The [second](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J008100) describes his clever lyrics and delivery:

    Allison’s songs have become popular on account of their ironic, often biting wit; among the finest are [*Parchman Farm*](https://open.spotify.com/track/3njG0Ys4tBoCLvAQVkQPrg?si=nOzREp2KR12nZ3FSIieBOA) and *Tell Me Something* , which he performed in a sophisticated, blues-inflected style.

    Allison experienced a resurgence of popularity in the 1980s. For an example of his later career, check out [“My Brain”](https://open.spotify.com/track/5TbL2yYGvicMC1aLAJsOjr?si=ErCH1DrtR4uncuSGSvfC4w) from 2010.

    Here's [the Grove Music Online article about Joe Williams](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J485400) . Note the following points:

    From 1954 to 1961 he was a member of Basie’s big band, where his dramatic performance of ballads and powerful blues singing were an immediate success; among the many hit recordings he made with the group is *Every Day I Have the Blues* (1955).

    With his rich bass-baritone voice and passionate style of delivery, Williams reshaped the role of the big-band singer and brought it up to date without sacrificing his innate taste and musical imagination.

    Listen to [this episode of NPR's *Fresh Air* about Williams](https://freshairarchive.org/segments/remembering-joe-williams) . It features an interview with him from 1989.
