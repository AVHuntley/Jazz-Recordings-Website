heading: John Coltrane
intro: |-
  John William Coltrane (1926-1967)

  His career saw him rise from little-known and “not entirely popular” sideman in Miles Davis’s first great quintet to perhaps the most influential jazz musician ever at the time of his death. [(Porter, p.300)](bibliography.html#porter-ullman-1993)

  Practiced constantly – obsessively.

  Used extremely open mouthpiece.

  He was extremely popular in his lifetime. *A Love Supreme* was a worldwide hit. [(Porter, p.302)](bibliography.html#porter-ullman-1993)

  Attended Ornstein School of Music in Philadelphia, then drafted into Navy (1945).

  Hired into Dizzy’s big band in 1949. Also played in small groups with Dizzy. Played with Johnny Hodges (alto sax w/ Ellington) small band. Played with Art Blakey in 1957.

  Influenced by Charlie Parker, Coleman Hawkins, Lester Young, Dexter Gordon, Wardell Gray, Sonny Stitt (on tenor especially).

  Joined Miles Davis in 1955. Davis fired Coltrane in 1957 because Coltrane was using drugs too heavily. Coltrane played with Thelonious Monk for a while, then Davis hired Coltrane again in 1958 after Coltrane had cleaned up. Coltrane left in 1960.

- heading: Sheets of Sound
  prose: |-
    **Sheets of Sound:** A style of playing associated with Coltrane in which he employs extremely dense lines consisting of high-speed arpeggios and scale patterns played in rapid succession: hundreds of notes running from the lowest to highest registers. The lines are often faster than sixteenth notes, consisting of quintuplets, septuplets, etc., and can sound like glissandos.

- heading: Change Running & Coltrane Changes
  prose: |-
    **Coltrane changes:** harmony that employs root movement of major and minor thirds, often as a substitute for more traditional harmony.

    **Change Running:** soloing that consists almost entirely of digital patterns that explicitly outline the chords.

- heading: Modal
  prose: |-
    Coltrane demonstrates a slightly different take on modal jazz than Davis did. He uses single chords for long stretches but plays much more rapid lines.

    Coltrane and Tyner both use chords and melodic patterns built on perfect fourths (and their inversions).
