intro: |-
  [Grove Music Online](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J752800) describes **post bop** as:

  A vague term, used either stylistically or chronologically (with divergent results) to describe any continuation or amalgamation of bop, modal jazz, and free jazz; its meaning sometimes extends into swing and earlier styles or into fusion and third-world styles. It emerged as an attempt to circumscribe the eclecticism which has characterized jazz from the 1980s onwards.

  Most commonly, the term **post bop** refers to jazz from the mid-1960s and onward that blends a lot of the elements that we covered in Unit 3: hard bop, modal jazz, and free jazz. Although the term can refer to a variety of jazz music that came after bebop, it most commonly describes the music of Miles Davis’s Second Great Quintet and John Coltrane’s Classic Quartet.

  Here's one way I think of post bop compositions: take the interesting non-functional harmonies of modal jazz but play them with the harmonic rhythms of hard bop. That is to say, play interesting colorful chords, but instead of holding each one out for long periods of time, play the chords like it was a hard bop tune, averaging one or two chords per measure. The harmonies might relate to each other, but not in the typical Great American Songbook kind of way, with ii-V-I progressions moving to closely-related keys.

  In post bop performance practice, the rhythm section plays with about as much freedom as the soloist does. It's a constant dance between the members of the rhythm section and the soloist they accompany. In fact, sometimes the rhythm section functions *as* the soloist. Take, for example, the Miles Davis tune " [Nefertiti](https://open.spotify.com/track/6AanQLrt4GtYXutqNiiQJG?si=zP-8KOsiTKKErPyFUdxgfA) ." Wayne Shorter and Miles Davis play the same melody over and over throughout the entire track. Behind them, Herbie Hancock, Carter, and Williams improvise ever-different ways of accompanying, and *that accompaniment* becomes the focus.

  Soloists are also free to do almost whatever they want. One biographer of Davis's quotes Hancock describing Davis's thoughts on soloing during this time:

  By the time we got to *E.S.P.* , Miles said, "I don't want to play chords any more"... I guess what he wanted to go for was the core of the music... Here's how I look at it... now I don't know if this is the way Miles looks at it, but a composition is an example of a conception, so Miles, rather than play the composition, he wants to play the conception that the composition came from... that's why you hear melody fragments and you kind of hear the momentum and the sound of the tune somewhere - something that distinguishes that tune from another one... but maybe the chords are not there. Even when we were playing "Walkin'" or any of those other [familiar] things, he didn't want to play the chords after we played the melody. ([Herbie Hancock, quoted by Ian Carr](https://books.google.com/books?id=bE_JBgAAQBAJ&pg=PA201&lpg=PA201&dq=miles+davis+%22I+don%27t+want+to+play+chords%22&source=bl&ots=b9PJdd40Ah&sig=ACfU3U3C_16yC-T4Vare7EULP1vtQiwzMw&hl=en&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwiko8Hmi6foAhUQ26wKHdguDqEQ6AEwAHoECAgQAQ#v=onepage&q=miles%20davis%20%22I%20don't%20want%20to%20play%20chords%22&f=false))

  Some other points about post bop, and mainstream jazz from this period:

  - Forms, tempos, and meters are freer. The musicians just listen to each other really well.
  - Compositions tend to be new. There's less of an emphasis on playing standards.
  - Band members themselves are often featured composers.
  - Drummers play fluidly, often challenging their band mates. Tony Williams often doesn't emphasize any particular beat, so it can be hard to tell where the "one" is, if you get lost.
  - Many of the post-bop artists of the 1960 became fusion artists in the 1970s.

    We've already discussed Miles Davis in great detail in [Miles](%24WIKI_REFERENCE%24/pages/3-dot-1-miles "Miles") , and we even listened to "E.S.P.," which is a classic post bop recording. I want you to hear one more track by this group, because a recording exists of them figuring out their arrangement in the studio before recording the final version.

    So, first, listen to [the original recording of "Freedom Jazz Dance"](https://open.spotify.com/track/0KciFkq67qqNrfmvbX1FCR?si=74N4kUMhRD2epki9JtVKow) by Eddie Harris. It's hardly abstract; it grooves! Note the part played by the piano and the way the melody is strung together. Then observe how, during the solos, the rhythm section pretty much keeps playing the same parts.

    Second, listen to the Miles Davis quintet discover their arrangment together on a track labeled " [Session Reel](https://open.spotify.com/track/4V0AcmpruhZKAYWTlrlVMw?si=Atyt2Se8QOCdL4HcJjfO4Q) ."

    - 0:34 - Ron Carter works out a way to play that piano part on the bass.
    - 4:00 - The band tries playing it through for the first time.
    - 5:00 - Hancock works out some voicings to use.
    - 7:00 - Tony Williams starts finding some stuff on the drums, incorporating pitched blocks.
    - 10:00 - Davis practices soloing.
    - 11:17 - Davis admits he can't play the head on the trumpet. He says to Wayne Shorter, "We've got to divide this up... I can't play it all!"
    - 13:00 - Davis suggests breaking the melody up into three phrases - and that's how they end up doing it on the recording. Just like that, Miles Davis influences how several generations of musicians play the tune.
    - 20:20 - Davis suggests that Williams try playing triplets on the snare throughout. At 21:08, Williams says he "can't play [triplets] that fast!"

    Finally, listen to the finished product.

    Oliver Nelson was a saxophone player, composer, and arranger. His [Grove Music Online entry](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2242494) notes his move to New York in 1959, where he "made a series of highly regarded recordings, many of which included his own compositions. The most important of these was the album *The Blues and the Abstract Truth.* " Nelson was also an in-demand arranger who worked with "Wes Montgomery, ....Count Basie, Lee Morgan, and Buddy Rich, among others."

    He published a jazz education book called [*Patterns for Improvisation*](https://www.alfred.com/patterns-for-improvisation/p/24-PI/) .

    Listen to (or read the transcript of) [this short NPR radio segment about Oliver Nelson's album *The Blues and the Abstract Truth*](https://www.npr.org/2011/06/20/4192723/oliver-nelson-the-blues-and-the-abstract-truth) . They discuss  a few noteworthy things about this famous album. They describe Eric Dolphy's participation in the record, playing bass clarinet and flute. And they describe Oliver Nelson's compositional influences:

    SPELLMAN: I think that you can hear in Nelson's writing his influences, which included the Duke Ellington/Billy Strayhorn group, as well as the compositions of Quincy Jones. I think one thing he shared with Quincy Jones was the ability to make a very small group sound large. So you hear the opening lines of "Stolen Moments" which we played a bit earlier, and you think that you're listening to a much larger band than you're hearing.

    They also discuss the all-star rhythm section: Bill Evans (p), Paul Chambers (b), Roy Haynes (d).

- heading: Herbie Hancock
  artists:
  - name: Herbie Hancock
    born: 1940
  prose: |-
    Herbert Jeffrey Hancock, was the pianist in the Second Great Quintet, and he's had an incredibly diverse and successful career. He began his solo career while working with Miles Davis and, like Davis, Hancock has been a musical innovator in several styles. His first few solo albums show the various influences of (at least) [hard bop](https://open.spotify.com/track/4Vyq3McGW0wr0sGLHYXys7?si=Hl851qgKQgijxls5Kr2-8Q) , [Cuban jazz](https://open.spotify.com/track/7asJDWkIZl6r0Nc12JyrAS?si=y8HgVlpoTW-RcNFBeAhv6g) , and [post bop](https://open.spotify.com/track/1Ku5vu9Crncz8ixeT4Ijxc?si=sK4NblwATc-wmekcbuyScA) . Later recordings include [groovy soul music](https://open.spotify.com/track/4K4S4huHJhTmbm0G8eF7rP?si=0NBCwWRzRdGQWWOZzA7glA) , [experimental fusion a la *Bitches Brew*](https://open.spotify.com/track/1wlEX6JootSgVOGby6gcYy?si=z8OMRGJVRpSAATSa5VdBSg) , [jazz-funk](https://open.spotify.com/track/1Aix42Q7mxGLMDglHhIOg3?si=MIjzJTnpQfSh_2lCMLkV3Q) , and [disco](https://open.spotify.com/track/0taRW8bPp2xW6ewm6PDMTZ?si=sPecw7DZQHOPeYHU2caXkA) . Hancock was never shy about creating accessible music. In the early 1980s, he won a Grammy for a very popular R&B track, " [Rockit](https://youtu.be/GHhD4PD75zY) ."

    Hancock, in his 80s, is still very musically active. He [still tours](https://www.herbiehancock.com/tour/) . For many years, he's been the director of the the [Herbie Hancock Institute of Jazz](https://hancockinstitute.org/) (formerly the Thelonious Monk Institute of Jazz). And in 2007, Hancock received the Grammy Award for Album of the Year for *[River: The Joni Letters](https://open.spotify.com/album/3GwR5VxaMzB01ay6qIT3Yq?si=OwvI1vVPQHWoGBslpIWnlQ)*
    - only the second ever awarded to a jazz album (the first was to [Getz/Gilberto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getz/Gilberto) in 1965)!

    Hancock describes in [his biography](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Herbie_Hancock/xejYCwAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=0) this song's origins. He was hired to write a song for a commercial depicting a sophisticated jazz club. He came up with the rhythm on an airplane while talking with Wayne Shorter. He wrote the rhythm down on a napkin then lost it. He remembered the rhythm at a recording session for “ [Eighty-One](https://open.spotify.com/track/0eyDYZmV5seBW1j9Ga41AR?si=XIDPDCnDQvaXvhSh-q5chQ) ” (you can hear him play it in the closing moments of that recordings). Later, he came up with the chords for it. His wife would not let him go to bed one night until he finished it and that’s when he realized, “the first two chords should also be the last two chords… The song would be structured like a spiral.” (90)

    Hancock has said that "Maiden Voyage" is his favorite of all his compositions. (I think. I can't find the source for this right right now, but I'll update this text when I do.)

    We've already been exposed to Freddie Hubbard in many contexts. He played on every recording covered on this page except for Miles Davis's. In 1960, he played with Coltrane on Ascension.
