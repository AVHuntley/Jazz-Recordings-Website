intro: |-
  The big band format reached peak popularity during the swing era, sometimes called the "big band era." But tastes changed with the times, and big bands did not flourish after about 1945. Many went out of business as jazz became less popular. Ted Gioia details the many factors that led to the diminishment of the big band in his book *The History of Jazz* :

  By 1950, the big band was effectively dead as a major force in American popular music. In the years following World War II, a number of social and economic factors conspired to force the big bands slowly, but inexorably, out of existence. A tax on dance venues, later repealed, may have been the first wedge separating jazz musicians from their mass audience. In the postwar years, the cost of promoting swing music further escalated as the price of transportation, wages, and hotel rooms rose steadily. An increasingly foreboding reputation for jazz as a music for serious listeners only, a legacy of the beboppers, clearly dates from this period as well. In time, the spread of television not only encouraged people to stay at home but distracted the public's attention from radio, which had provided free advertising for jazz music in the previous two decades. But swing music itself was also partly to blame, its vital core enervated by the formulaic gestures of the name bands and the soundalike sameness of the second-tier groups. The public's taste was now shifting to pop singers, such as Frank Sinatra, and soon would embrace the transgressive sounds of rock and roll. Perhaps no single factor was pivotal in killing the big band, but the cumulative impact was enormous. In December 1946, no fewer than eight major swing orchestras disbanded—including those of [Benny] Goodman, [Harry] James, [Tommy] Dorsey, and [Jack] Teagarden. By the close of the decade, only a few surviving units continued to work with any regularity. [(Gioia, p.179)](https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_History_of_Jazz/J9G50L3c14QC?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22by%201950,%20the%20big%20band%20was%20effectively%20dead%22)

  Among the few swing era band leaders who managed to keep working during the 50s and 60s were Duke Ellington, Count Basie, Maynard Ferguson, Stan Kenton, Woody Herman, and Buddy Rich. In fact, there's a wonderful story about Duke Ellington's resurgence. His career, like that of most other big band leaders, was experiencing a downturn, but a [very well-received performance at the 1956 Newport Jazz Festival](https://open.spotify.com/album/0t41BkcZayaAsa0FdRelfz?si=lrNAXvwUQiOPOT9cHl0Gvw) revitalized it. That legendary story is told well in [this article from The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/music/musicblog/2010/apr/06/duke-ellington-newport-concert) :

  ...it wasn't until Ellington called the tenor saxophonist Paul Gonsalves to blow on an old 1937 arrangement – rekindled for the occasion as [Diminuendo and Crescendo in Blue](https://open.spotify.com/track/1bwU4f1TYZksvSA4Rfia1p?si=WRBvRXAySYulAWxk8syCLw) – that the event took off in a manner none of the participants could ever have predicted.

  In an electrifying performance by the band, Gonsalves – who hadn't played the piece in a while, and was initially uncertain of his way around it – played 27 improvised choruses in a raunchily R&B and gospel-inflected manner.

  Ellington's faltering reputation was rejuvenated overnight. The show won one of the loudest ovations in Newport festival history, and on the strength of it the bandleader made the cover of Time...

  One way big bands managed to stay relevant was by becoming increasingly associated with high schools and colleges. Stan Kenton, in particular, associated himself with colleges - Kenton Hall at UNT is named after him. And many of the bandleaders from this unit taught at universities.

  Increasingly, big bands played music from outside of the jazz canon. And - like many jazz groups of the time - they incorporated elements from popular music such as electronic instruments and rock grooves.

  ## Vocabulary
  **Rehearsal band** : “A big band that meets for enjoyment and practice, or to play new compositions and arrangements, only occasionally performing in front of an audience; (now also) a smaller group of musicians that meet informally to play big band music.” ([Oxford Dictionary](https://www.lexico.com/definition/rehearsal_band))

    Gerald Wilson was a Los Angeles-based trumpeter player, big band leader, composer, and arranger. Read the [Grove Music Online article about Gerald Wilson](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J488300) . It describes his early career working with Jimmy Lunceford, Benny Carter, Count Basie, and Duke Ellington. He led his first big band from 1944 to 1947. Then:

    In 1961 Wilson formed a new band which recorded regularly until 1969 and gave many successful performances, most notably at the Monterey Jazz Festival in 1963. The orchestra was widely acclaimed, both for the caliber of its players... and for the quality of Wilson’s compositions.

    His 1960s big band released 11 albums on the Pacific Label during the decade, most of which are well-reviewed. Like many groups from this era, Wilson's occasionally covered popular (non-jazz) material. For example, their 1968 album [California Soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Soul_(album)) included covers of (then) contemporary rock songs: "Light My Fire" (The Doors), "Sunshine of Your Love" (Cream), "California Soul" (The Messengers).

    Wilson was faculty at several universities around Los Angeles: CSU Los Angeles, UCLA, Cal State Northridge, and CalArts. In 1990, he was named an [NEA Jazz Master](https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/gerald-wilson) . His NEA page mentions the influence of Mexican culture on his music, perhaps related to his wife of more than 50 years, who was Mexican-American:

    His series of Pacific Jazz recordings established his unique harmonic voice, and Mexican culture—especially the bullfight tradition—influenced his work.

Quincy Jones arranged and/or produced them! Jones, nicknamed "Q," is a hit maker in many genres. But his origins are in jazz.

    Here are some basic biographical details. He was born in Chicago and raised in Seattle. He met Ray Charles when they were both young teenagers. He befriended Clark Terry, who was then working in Count Basie's band. He attended what was then called the Schillinger House (now known as the Berklee School of Music). He played for a while with Lionel Hampton's big band before forming his own and touring across Europe. Later he worked for Mercury Records, where he produced "It's My Party" for Leslie Gore. He went on to work with Michael Jackson, producing some of the most influential pop music of the century.

    One of the hallmarks of Jones's jazz arrangements is his use of flutes and muted trumpets, a texture that became increasingly popular in the 1960s.

    Read the [Grove Music Online article on Buddy Rich](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.23374) . Buddy Rich was a drummer and bandleader who had a long career in music. He played with several big bands during the swing era, including that of Tommy Dorsey from 1932-1942. Rich also led his own group during that period. Then, in the 1960s, he formed a new band in Los Angeles. As the Grove Music Online article notes:

    In 1966 he organized a second big band, with which he achieved remarkable international success until its dissolution in 1974.

    Rich's playing was characterized by phenomenal speed and dexterity. He was an extrovert performer who produced complex patterns with metronomic clarity and simpler lines with an exquisite precision. Although he was a product of the 1930s Swing Era, Rich easily adapted to later jazz styles. In 1950 he participated in [a recording session with bebop pioneers Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk and Charlie Parker](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:5331/tjd/ReleaseDetail?c=Verve&C=831133-2#R107992) . He was also among the first jazz musicians of an earlier period to include rock elements in his playing and band arrangements. He recorded Lennon and McCartney's [*Norwegian Wood*](https://open.spotify.com/track/0LEmKTCO9XusACZBhMNoe1?si=eMmdnTR-SbS0tIIXE2HVFA) in early 1967 and, to achieve an authentic jazz rock sound, used an electric rather than acoustic bass in his later bands.

    The musicians in his 1966 band were largely graduates of North Texas State University (now UNT) and Berkelee. [(Sultanof, p.140)](bibliography.html#sultanof-2017)

  prose: |-
    Donald Johnson Ellis was a Los Angeles-based trumpeter, composer, drummer, and bandleader. [This page from UNC](https://www.uncjazzpress.com/category-s/161.htm) provides a nice, short summary of his legacy (and links to purchase many of his charts):

    Don Ellis led some of the most colorful big bands of the 1966-78 period. Ellis's big bands were distinguished by their unusual instrumentation, the exploitation of unusual time signatures, its occasional humor and openness towards using rock rhythms and electronics. His orchestra achieved enormous popular appeal at a time when the influence of big band music was noticeably fading. Ellis applied his knowledge of the music of non-Western cultures to the rhythmic language of jazz. He was one of the first to have accomplished such a fusion of ideas, and his works stand as a memorial reflecting a significant stage in the evolution of jazz.

    Read the [Grove Music Online article about Ellis](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2228242) . Note what it says about his musical style in the paragraphs following this line:

    Ellis is often associated with Ornette Coleman, John Coltrane, and Cecil Taylor as a proponent of the avant-garde jazz of the late 1950s and early 1960s. However, he generally avoided a radical free-form style...

    Note too where the article discusses the influence of Indian music on Ellis. He experimented with complex meters, quarter notes, and non-Western scales. The article notes:

    Ellis’s interest in oriental music is illustrated by later compositions, such as Indian Lady, which begins in the manner of a raga.

    Ellis, like many musicians of the time, also experimented with electronic instruments. He often played his trumpet through effects - amplifiers, ring modulators, and echoes (he sometimes referred to this setup as his "electrophonic trumpet"). Thus the sound of his band embodied many of the qualities of late-60s fusion: electric timbres and rock rhythms, played with a spirit of adventurous, avant-garde experimentalism.

    There's a good biography of Maynard Ferguson on his [official website](https://maynardferguson.com/biography/) . It details his early recognition as a child prodigy, first as a classical violinist then as a trumpeter. He moved from his home in Montreal, Canada to the United States to play with Stan Kenton, in whose band he played for about 5 years. He left Kenton's band to pursue studio work. He played in the Paramount Pictures studio orchestra and was featured on “at least forty-six soundtracks.” [(Sultanof, p.128)](bibliography.html#sultanof-2017)

    He also frequently led his own big bands, which gained some attention with their recordings of popular songs of the day, including "MacArthur Park" (below), " [Gonna Fly Now"](https://open.spotify.com/track/3kptOaH2SE1lbgKm3Umhf6?si=OX6db0EUTtay8VOuXBIKpQ) (1976, from the movie Rocky), and the Michael Jackson song " [Don't Stop 'Til You Get Enough](https://open.spotify.com/track/1GpvcxlkZn9Pe63TF1qfRk?si=Va6vtTuZTJy5Drh8-8A5gw) " (1979).

    Ferguson was known, among other things, for his exceptional ability to play high notes with good tone and excellent pitch control. Non-horn players should understand that this is quite an athletic feat. To some, this can seem a bit gimmicky. When Jerome Kern's widow heard [Ferguson's playing on Kern's classic "All the Things You Are,"](http://chirb.it/fmNvaN) it upset her so much that the recording was withdrawn from sale
