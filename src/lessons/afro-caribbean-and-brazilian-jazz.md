vocabulary:
- term: Salsa
  definition: A catch-all term for Afro-Caribbean dance music. Designed primarily for dancing, often features a vocalist and horn section.
- term: Clave
  definition: A two-part rhythmic pattern (e.g., son or rumba clave) that organizes the entire ensemble. It can be in 2-3 or 3-2 order. Aligning parts with the correct side of the clave is crucial.
- term: Tumbao
  definition: A repeating bass (and sometimes piano/percussion) pattern in Afro-Caribbean music, emphasizing offbeats (like the “and” of 2 and 4).
- term: Montuno
  definition: A repeating piano pattern or vamp used in Afro-Caribbean music, often in the song’s later “open” section.
- term: Cruzado
  definition: Playing rhythms that contradict the established clave, creating tension.
- term: Moña
  definition: A horn line or layered horn riff section in Afro-Caribbean music, often appearing during a montuno or mambo section.
- term: Bossa Nova
  definition: A Brazilian style that emerged in the late 1950s, blending samba with a quieter, more subdued aesthetic, featuring guitar, soft vocals, and complex harmonies.
intro: |-
  The term "Latin jazz" is often used broadly to describe a wide range of music that blends jazz with various Latin American musical traditions. For practical purposes, jazz musicians typically differentiate between two major streams of “Latin” influence:

  - Afro-Caribbean (especially Cuban-based) music
  - Brazilian (especially bossa nova and samba-derived) music

  Understanding the differences between these two streams is crucial. If someone calls for a "Latin" groove on a gig, you should know whether they mean a Cuban-based style (like a salsa feel) or a Brazilian style (like a bossa nova). They sound, feel, and are structured differently.

  Historically, Cuban and Caribbean influences have been part of jazz since its early days in New Orleans. Jelly Roll Morton famously referenced the "Spanish tinge," which was actually more Afro-Caribbean than European Spanish. Brazilian music, particularly bossa nova, surged in popularity in the U.S. during the early 1960s, influenced by artists like Stan Getz, João Gilberto, and Antônio Carlos Jobim. Bossa nova’s more subtle, “cool” approach contrasts with the often more energetic Afro-Caribbean grooves.

  In Afro-Caribbean styles (such as Cuban-based salsa), the *clave* rhythm is central. All parts align with a 2-3 or 3-2 clave pattern, and instruments like congas, timbales, and bongos combine with piano montunos and bass tumbaos. On the other hand, Brazilian bossa nova often features gentle guitar and vocal melodies over a softly syncopated but generally more understated rhythm. The bass line in bossa nova is simpler compared to the syncopated tumbao of Cuban music.

  Let’s also recall that “salsa” is often a marketing term. Afro-Caribbean jazz and salsa share similar rhythmic foundations, but salsa is generally more dance-oriented, while “Latin jazz” leans more toward listening and improvisation in a jazz setting.
