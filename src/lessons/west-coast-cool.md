intro: |-
  Some characteristics of "cool" jazz:

  - Toned-down dynamics; drummers might play with brushes, brass players might use mutes, trumpet players may instead play the mellower flugelhorn.
  - Tempos, on average, are slower than those in bebop
  - There's often a greater emphasis on listenable, sing-able, melodies than on fast, technical lines.
  - Trumpets usually play in the middle register - not really high, like Gillespie.
  - Horn players associated with "cool" jazz tend to use less vibrato than those associated with bop did. (And bop players tended to use less vibrato than swing players did)
  - Fewer blues and rhythm changes tunes.
  - Larger ensembles often use instruments atypical in jazz: French horn, tuba, flute, oboe.
  - Arrangements often feature counterpoint.

  “First home was in a Los Angeles [Hermosa Beach] club called The Lighthouse” [(Lyons, p.167)](bibliography.html#lyons-1980)

  Perhaps Frankie Trumbauer and Bix Beiderbecke (Chicago-style Dixieland jazz) were progenitors of the cool aesthetic.

  [Episode 8 of the The Ken Burns jazz documentary](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:4498/watch/risk) includes a nice summary of "cool" jazz: **1:33:06 – 1:34:29**

    Baritone sax player, arranger, and composer. He wrote and arranged for Claude Thornhill, Stan Kenton, and Miles Davis on *Birth of the Cool* (“Jeru” from that album is a Mulligan original).

    “Their unique instrumentation was discovered by accident in 1952, when pianist Jimmy Rowles failed to show up for a private recording session being taped by Richard Bock.” [(Lyons, p.167)](bibliography.html#lyons-1980)

  prose: |-
    From Oklahoma. While in the Army, he was relocated to San Francisco, where he played in local jazz clubs.

    Baker played a tour with Charlie Parker, then was part of Mulligan’s piano-less quartet. These gigs made him popular. Unfortunately, Mulligan’s group with Baker lasted for less than one year because Baker was arrested on drug charges.

- heading: Dave Brubeck
  artists:
  - name: Dave Brubeck
    born: 1920
    died: 2012
  prose: |-
    [Burns "Jazz" Episode 8](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:4498/watch/risk) features an informative interview with Brubeck about the following two tracks, beginning at **1:34:28**

    Born in Concord, CA, lived in Amador County.

    Brubeck can sound kind of heavy handed. This is perhaps in part because of swimming accident. In 1951, while vacationing in Hawaii, Brubeck accidentally dove into a hidden sand bar, injuring his neck. He describes being almost paralyzed for a few years; he lost some dexterity and so relied on block chords for a while. This became a trademark of his sound. (Gioia, *West Coast Jazz* , 89)

    He attended the University of the Pacific in Stockton, CA for veterinary science. A professor advised him to switch to music. When the school found out that he couldn't read music, he was almost expelled! Today, UoP is home to the [Dave Brubeck Institute](https://www.pacific.edu/brubeck-home.html) .

    Brubeck was in the army in 1942 but was spared from combat because he played piano at a show and was a hit. He later studied at Mills College in Oakland.

    Brubeck formed his quartet in 1951 with Paul Desmond (as). They gained popularity playing on college campuses.

    He was on the cover of Time Magazine 1954, somewhat controversially among jazz musicians, because he was only second jazz musician ever to be featured there (the first was Louis Armstrong in February 1949)... before Ellington, Charlie Parker, and many other important African-American musicians.

- heading: Jim Hall
  artists:
  - name: Jim Hall
    born: 1930
    died: 2013
  prose: |-
    James Stanley Hall

    Born in Buffalo, NY. Grew up in Cleveland, OH. Attended the Cleveland Institute of Music, majoring in composition. Moved to Los Angeles, CA in 1956, where he was active for a few years in the West Coast scene.

    Performed with Chet Baker, Paul Desmond, Art Farmer.

    Recorded two duo albums with Bill Evans: *Undercurrent* (1962) and *Intermodulation* (1966).

    "It could be argued that the jazz guitar tree is rooted in four names: Django, Charlie, Wes, and Jim." ([Obituary by *Premier Guitar*](https://www.premierguitar.com/articles/20043-remembering-jim-hall-1930-2013))

    Hall emphasized the importance of spontaneity and originality in performance.

    "Players should force themselves to hear something and then play it rather than just do whatever comes under their fingers. I try to make my playing as fresh as possible by not relying on set patterns.

    When I practice, I tie off some of the strings with rubber bands to force myself to look at the fingerboard differently. For instance, I might play on the D and G strings only, or even just on the G and A strings." (Hall quoted on [JazzGuitarLessons.com](https://www.jazzguitarlessons.net/jim-hall/) )
