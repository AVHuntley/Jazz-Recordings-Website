intro: |-
  Before we get into the artists for this unit, let's briefly discuss some general characteristics of contemporary jazz and how it contrasts with jazz of prior decades. It’s challenging to describe the era in which we live, as we lack historical hindsight. Current practices may be fleeting or may shape the future of jazz. In preparing this lesson, I chose musicians who I believe may be recognized decades from now, though only time will tell.

  Contemporary jazz musicians often balance two aesthetic goals: forging an original voice and honoring the tradition. We admire innovators who don’t merely clone past masters. Yet we also value the canon of standards and centuries-old jazz vocabulary. The best contemporary musicians blend these impulses—innovation and tradition—and in doing so, gain critical acclaim.

  As we listen, note how musicians blend their influences. Which traditions remain intact? What new elements appear? How do they personalize their music while continuing the century-long conversation that is jazz?

  Some characteristics of modern jazz you might observe:

  - Odd time signatures are common. Whereas Brubeck’s *Time Out* was radical for its day, now meters like 5/4, 7/8, and complex polyrhythms are standard fare.
  - Musicians are more versatile:
    - Pianists may use acoustic piano, electric pianos, and synthesizers without remark.
    - Saxophonists double on multiple woodwinds.
    - Trumpeters must play lead, section parts, and solo with equal skill.
    - Drummers handle swing, rock, waltz, ECM, bossa nova, samba, salsa, hip-hop, and more.
  - Genres blend freely. A contemporary jazz piece might draw on multiple styles simultaneously.
  - Electronic and acoustic instruments coexist naturally. Synths and electric guitars appear in various contexts.
  - Unusual instrument combinations are normal (e.g., accordion in Maria Schneider’s band).
  - Covering contemporary pop songs is no longer unusual. Just as swing and bebop artists once transformed their era’s popular tunes into jazz standards, today’s artists do the same.
  - Universities have become crucial training grounds for jazz musicians. Whereas earlier generations learned primarily in the community, formal jazz education is now widespread.

    Read the Grove Music Online article about [Brad Mehldau](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2276098) . He emerged in the spotlight as part of Joshua Redman’s quartet. His own career includes the acclaimed *Art of the Trio* series. Mehldau is celebrated for covering popular music (Radiohead, Elliot Smith, Nick Drake, and more) and for his remarkable left-hand independence, weaving contrapuntal lines reminiscent of classical fugues.

    Mehldau’s style shows influences of Romantic-era composers like Brahms and Schubert. His original compositions often bear a "Sturm und Drang" aesthetic, lending a dramatic, introspective quality.

- heading: Robert Glasper
  artists:
  - name: Robert Glasper
    born: 1978
  prose: |-
    Read [Glasper’s Grove Music article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2228319) . From Houston, TX, and a New School alum, Glasper worked with Christian McBride, Kenny Garrett, Roy Hargrove, and others. He became known for blending hip hop and jazz. His early albums were acoustic trios with modern mainstream jazz traits. Later albums introduced his fusion group Experiment, merging jazz, soul, and hip hop. He often draws on repeated motives that evoke a DJ’s turntable technique.

    Glasper’s *Black Radio* albums won acclaim and Grammys. He’s also explored J Dilla’s beats in his “Dillalude” tracks.

  prose: |-
    Read the short [Grove Music Online article about Jason Moran](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2242299) . Also from Houston, Moran studied at the Manhattan School of Music. He currently teaches at the New England Conservatory.

    In 2010, Moran received a MacArthur "genius" fellowship. He blends historical jazz references with modern sensibilities. He has honored Thelonious Monk’s music and worked on tributes to Fats Waller, melding stride tradition with contemporary rhythms.

    Read [Iyer's Grove Music article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2241782) . Iyer, born to Indian immigrants in New York, studied math and physics at Yale, then earned a Ph.D. from UC Berkeley focused on music cognition. He now teaches at Harvard.
