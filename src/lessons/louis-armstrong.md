    died: 1971
    portrait: https://tile.loc.gov/image-services/iiif/public:music:musgottlieb-00171-002:0001/full/pct:50.0/0/default.jpg
    portraitCredit: 'William P. Gottlieb, [LOC](https://www.loc.gov/collections/gottlieb/).'
  prose: |-
    Born in New Orleans. Came from a difficult background. Eventually found himself running errands for his idol, King Oliver. Got some lessons in return.

    In 1917, when Oliver moved to Chicago, Armstrong took over for Oliver in Kid Ory's band. Eventually he was hired by Oliver as 2nd cornet and moved to Chicago (where he influenced Bix Beiderbecke).

    In 1924, he moved to New York to join Fletcher Henderson's band.

    In 1925, he returned to Chicago. Between 1925 and 1928, he recorded the Hot Five and Hot Seven sessions.

    Here, he met and became close with pianist Earl “Fatha” Hines (1903-1983).

    Armstrong is often called the first great jazz soloist. He was rhythmically innovative, a natural syncopator. He ornamented and embellished melodies.

    **Scat** singing is vocal improvisation with wordless vocables, meaningless syllables, or without words at all.

    intro: |-
      Recorded in Chicago.

      Form: **32-bar AB** (or ABAC). No head; just solos.

      Changes based on “Bill Bailey.” Same changes as the chorus of “Tiger Rag,” but Armstrong played “Tiger Rag” in a different key.

      Less emphasis on ensemble playing. Armstrong (or Dodds, though less so) is in front.

      The Hot Five usually included Johnny St. Cyr on guitar. Johnson is a guest star here. Lil Hardin Armstrong and Johnson both play four-to-the-floor chords.

      There's a nice [blog post about this track](http://dippermouth.blogspot.com/2010/07/hotter-than-that_09.html). [p. 60](bibliography.html#bierman-2016)

      The "West End" of the title refers to the westernmost point of Lake Pontchartrain in Orleans Parish, Louisiana. In its heyday, it was a thriving summer resort with live music, dance pavilions, seafood restaurants, and lake bathing.

      Singleton is audible in the 2nd chorus only. He plays on wooden blocks.

      Earl Hines was a friend of, and frequent collaborator with, Armstrong. Hines was said to have played in a “trumpet style.”

      Among those who were heavily influenced by the opening of this recording is Charlie Parker. He quoted that opening on more than one occasion. [This blog post](http://jazzontherecord.blogspot.com/2018/08/bird-quotes-satchmo.html) documents a few of those times.
