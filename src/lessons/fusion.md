intro: |-
  The term "fusion" in the context of jazz history most often refers to the blending of jazz and rock styles that began in the late 1960s and reached a zenith in the 1970s. Read the [Grove Music Online article about jazz-rock fusion](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J226300) . It provides an excellent summary of some key elements of the style. Note especially the following points from the second paragraph:

  In the 1970s three key elements defined a jazz-rock performance. One was the presence of amplified and electronic instruments... The second was the presence of a rock or funk rhythm, which emphasizes a heavy backbeat and has [straight eighths]... rather than the triplet-eighth phrasing of swing... The third was the presence of jazz harmony, either in improvisation or accompaniment. A fourth but less crucial element was rock and funk form.

  Let's breakdown these four elements:

  - **Electronic instruments** allowed musicians to play louder and to more easily manipulate timbre. Guitarists played electric guitars through powerful, distortion-inducing amps. Pianists often played on electro-acoustic pianos like the [Fender Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes_piano) and the [Wurlizter electric piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurlitzer_electric_piano) . Electric guitars, pianos, and even horns could be run through effects like [wah-wah pedals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wah-wah_pedal) and [echoplexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echoplex) And with the introduction of commercially-available synthesizers in the early 1970s (especially portable, affordable models like the legendary [Minimoog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimoog) ), pianists' roles were significantly expanded. Synthesizer enthusiasts like Joe Zawinul and Herbie Hancock were no longer just pianists; they were *keyboard artists* .
  - **Rock and funk rhythms** replaced the "spang-spang-a-lang" swing pattern of more traditional jazz styles. These beats were not necessarily danceable. Indeed, they were often incredibly sophisticated and poly-metric. But they had more in common with the raucous sounds of rock-and-roll than with the triplet-based patterns of swing era big bands .
  - **Jazz harmony and improvisation** were nevertheless major components of fusion - they're the *jazz* part of jazz-rock. Harmonies could be, at times, challenging. Sometimes harmony was only nominally-present, like on *Bitches Brew* , which borders on atonality.
  - **Rock and funk forms** often involve extended vamps over relatively static harmony. This may be just a single chord - drawing perhaps on the tradition of modal jazz - or a short progression of two to four chords.

  Several factors motivated jazz musicians' move to fusion, but in part it was a desire to stay relevant and sell albums. Avant-garde and post-bop jazz was not as accessible to general audiences as were the Grateful Dead, Jimi Hendrix, and Sly and the Family Stone. Perhaps the move to fusion was a motivated reaction to the increasingly abstract, art-for-art’s-sake jazz of the 1960s. Fusion musicians wanted to entertain.

  Miles Davis discusses his motivation in his autobiography, writing about the state of jazz in the 1960s:

  At the same time, people were packing stadiums to hear and see stars in person. And jazz music seemed to be withering on the vine, in record sales and live performances. It was the first time in a long time that I didn't sell out crowds everywhere I played... In the United States, we played to a lot of half empty clubs in 1969. That told me something. Compared to what my records used to sell, when you put them beside what Bob Dylan or Sly Stone sold, it was no contest. Their sales had gone through the roof. ([Davis, 297](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Miles/xgAVXHhuNYgC?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22At%20the%20same%20time,%20people%20were%20packing%20stadiums%20to%20hear%20and%20see%20stars%20in%20person%22))

  ...somewhere around this time, I started realizing that most rock musicians didn't know anything about music. They didn't study at, couldn't play different styles—and don't even talk about reading music. But they were popular and sold a lot of records because they were giving the public a certain sound, what they wanted to hear. So I figured if they could do it—reach all those people and sell all those records without really knowing what they were doing—then I could do it, too, only better. ([Davis, 302](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Miles/xgAVXHhuNYgC?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22somewhere%20around%20this%20time,%20I%20started%20realizing%20that%20wrote%20most%20rock%20musicians%20didn%27t%20know%20anything%20about%20music.%22))

  Because it moved away from certain aspects of jazz, and because it was sometimes enormously popular, fusion music and musicians often met with predictable scorn from cultural conservatives. Trumpeter Wynton Marsalis, for example, was a notable critic of fusion music. In a 1985 interview with Herbie Hancock, Marsalis leadingly asked, " we all agreed apparently at one point that jazz was more meaningful, in some sense, than pop music. Since you work in the two idioms, what do you feel is different? "

  Hancock, a consummate fusion artist, offered a poignant response:

  Wait a minute. I *don’t* agree. Let me address myself to that. When we have life, we have music. Music can be manifest in many different forms, and as long as they all have purpose they shouldn’t be pitted against each other as one being more important than the other. That’s stupid. (Quoted in [Fellez, *Birds of Fire*](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Birds_of_Fire/l6MQms7Wb0UC?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=Wait%20a%20minute.%20I%20don%E2%80%99t%20agree.%20Let%20me%20address%20myself%20to%20that) )

  In addition to rock and funk, fusion musicians also drew inspiration from the music of non-Western cultures. John McLaughlin of Mahavishnu Orchestra included elements of Indian music. Chick Corea's "Spain" drew from (you guessed it) Spanish influences. Broadly, though, jazz has always involved a degree of stylistic fusion. Blues, African rhythms, Latin rhythms, Broadway tunes, Spanish music, soul, and classical music have all influenced jazz. As Len Lyons puts it, " the idea of pure jazz is a myth. " [(Lyons, p.327)](bibliography.html#lyons-1980)

    Read the [Grove Music Online article about Mahavishnu Orchestra](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J285200) , then read [the article about its founder, John McLaughlin (1942-)](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J282500) . The Grove article notes the following features of the band:

    It was formed in summer 1971 by John McLaughlin; Mahavishnu was the spiritual name bestowed on McLaughlin by his guru... [The group's] music incorporated various elements: the biting electronic sound, sustained high volume, and dance rhythms of hard rock; the virtuoso improvisation and complex meters of jazz; and spiritual, mantra-like ostinatos borrowed from Indian religious traditions.

    An [article on the website *The Guitar Channel*](https://www.guitar-channel.com/rich_murray/mahavishnu-orchestra-40th-anniversary.html) offers the following colorful description of the band's influence and aesthetic:

    Though the Mahavishnu Orchestra didn’t invent jazz-rock, it was through its virtuosity that the music coalesced into a tangible genre...The Mahavishnu Orchestra’s 1973 album *Birds of Fire* even cracked the Billboard pop music charts reaching #15. Aggressive instrumental music like this had never been accepted in such a way.

    Thousands of fans showed up to fill large venues as the Mahavishnu Orchestra seemed to be on one long continuous tour. The music unrelentingly pushed out from the stage at gale force winds. The loud violence of it could, at times, threaten to separate you from your skeleton. Yet, there was a beauty to it as well. Listeners were left stunned in the wake of McLaughlin’s supersonic flights, Cobham’s power, Goodman’s soaring and Hammer’s deftness. All the while, bassist Laird somehow managed to keep everyone in some sort of line.

    McLaughlin played on a [custom double-necked guitar](http://www.theguitarcolumn.com/2009/08/john-mclaughlins-rex-bogue-double.html) that he called “Double Rainbow."

    Jan Hammer plays a synthesizer that sounds very similar to an electric guitar. In fact, Hammer often played a [keytar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keytar) .

- heading: Head Hunters
  prose: |-
    Herbie Hancock was featured in our lesson on post bop. Like many Miles Davis alums, Hancock forayed into fusion in the 1970s. His first fusion group - formally the Herbie Hancock Sextet but commonly Mwandishi - played more experimental works in the tradition of *Bitches Brew* . But that group didn't make much money, so in 1973 Hancock dissolved it and formed another: The Headhunters. Drawing inspiration from the Pointer Sisters and Sly and the Family Stone, The Headhunters played in a decidedly funk-influenced style.

    Their self-titled first album sold incredibly well. Within the first six months, *Head Hunters* sold more than 500,000 copies. Soon it surpassed Brubeck's *Take Five* to become the best-selling jazz record of all time ( *Kind of Blue* eventually caught up and surpassed that record).

    About the title of the group, Hancock writes that it is a triple entendre, representing " the jungle, the intellectual, and the sex " ([Hancock, *Possibilities* , 180](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Herbie_Hancock/xejYCwAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22the%20jungle,%20the%20intellectual,%20and%20the%20sex.%22))

- heading: Return to Forever
  prose: |-
    Chick Corea (1941-), along with Herbie Hancock, was one of the most influential pianists/keyboard artists of the 1960s and 1970s. Read about him on [his Grove Music Online article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J102200) . If you have some extra time, I also recommend [this two part documentary from Corea's YouTube channel](https://youtu.be/gM3CvSrHs60) .

    He was just as much at home playing the Fender Rhodes and Minimoog as he was the acoustic piano. And he was at least as influential on each. His [first recording](https://open.spotify.com/track/5Lvw556twbNWcsD0c7uaQX?si=6kcDuyktQ5a983bZb02hXA) was with Mongo Santamaria. His album *Now He Sings Now He Sobs* was widely recognized as a masterpiece of post bop jazz - listen to, for example, " [Matrix](https://open.spotify.com/track/13VdXFe27FOq9CW2CJaxID?si=0EblluzySgS8Kv6X_n6XkA) ." Corea replaced Hancock in Miles Davis's band in 1968, forming part of a group that has been dubbed "The Lost Quintet." Davis remembers this quintet fondly, writing:

    "Man, I wish this band had been recorded live because it was a really bad m\*\*\*\*\*f\*\*\*\*\*." ([Davis, 297](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Miles/xgAVXHhuNYgC?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22After%20we%20finished%20in%20a%20silent%20way%22))

    Lucky for Davis, [some recordings](https://open.spotify.com/album/4jSv7dCwUWqp8kf8nXBEGn?si=Iwq4f-8HTrWoiZ0bgxWukg) from this group did survive. You can read about and listen to the story of that album on [this NPR page](https://www.npr.org/2013/01/30/170662462/a-1969-bootleg-unearths-miles-davis-lost-quintet) . Corea also worked with Davis on *Bitches Brew* .

    Like many musicians of the late 1960s, Corea explored the avant-garde, but then wanted to move into more accessible territory. As Corea later remembered, " We took our way of thinking from Stockhausen, John Cage, and Cecil Taylor. But it got to the stage where we were sending our audiences up the river. That basic element, communication, was being left out. " [(Lyons, p.354)](bibliography.html#lyons-1980)

    Return to Forever is Chick Corea’s fusion band. It has existed in several forms since its founding in 1971, and Corea has recorded under that band name as recently as [2009](https://open.spotify.com/album/17D3HTU2Mr4vyEQB6JNmke?si=OsZbW19rRj-ehP3ZcKS8Hw) . The personnel has changed with each iteration but has always included Corea and bassist Stanley Clarke.

- heading: Weather Report
  prose: |-
    Read the short [Grove Music Online article about Weather Report](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:5982/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-2000476900) . This group was formed in 1970. Like Return to Forever, it underwent several personnel changes over the years, but had two consistent members: Wayne Shorter, who we discussed in our lesson on post bop , and Austrian keyboard artist Joe Zawinul, who we heard play with Cannonball Adderley in our lesson on soul jazz .

    The Grove article describes the sound of the group:

    Weather Report’s first albums provided several remarkable instances of unconventional collective playing, though their music remained accessible to a large audience. Discarding the traditional jazz roles of soloist and accompanist, the players took the lead by turn and created textures that were continuously changing; they elided tonal ostinato themes with improvisations and alternated unmetered passages with others underpinned by rock or Latin rhythms.

    In 1976, Jaco Pastorius took over the position as the bass player in Weather Report. Their first album with Pastorius, *Heavy Weather* , stands as one of the most significant and popular jazz fusion albums. “ [Birdland](https://open.spotify.com/track/19vVghIuHm3IAfN4xpRF3q?si=ol6WAu7sRQSRGIvRCKOfvw) ” was a huge hit single, and helped propel the album to #20 on the *Billboard 200* in 1977.

- heading: Steps Ahead
  prose: |-
    Steps Ahead was formed in 1979 by the vibraphonist Mike Mainieri, originally under the name "Steps." It featured saxophonist Michael Brecker (who we'll discuss further in the lesson on Other Soloists from 70s and 80s Other Soloists from 70s and 80s ), bassist Eddie Gomez (who played with Bill Evans in the 1970s), and drummer Peter Erskine (who remains an influential drummer and educator today). In 1983, their original keyboardist was replaced by Eliane Elias, who we heard in our lesson on Brazilian jazz .

    The Grove article on jazz-rock provided this description of Steps Ahead:

    For most of its history fusion was defined by its modernism, but in the 1980s the collision of neoclassical jazz and electric jazz gave birth to musical alliances of new and old. One of these was a fusion tempered by the past. An early example of this style was the work of Steps Ahead, a group formed in 1979 by Mike Mainieri and initially known simply as Steps. In its early years Steps Ahead used a conventional small-group lineup of tenor saxophone, vibraphone, piano, double bass, and drums, and, while it played some swinging jazz, it became best known for compositions which laid harmony and improvisations derived from 1960s jazz over rock, funk, and reggae beats; its style may be described as “acoustic fusion.”
