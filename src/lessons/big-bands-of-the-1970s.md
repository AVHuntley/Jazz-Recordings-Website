- heading: Gil Evans

  - name: Gil Evans

    We've already listened to Gil Evans in our study of [cool jazz big bands](%24WIKI_REFERENCE%24/pages/2-dot-5-cool-big-bands "2.5 - Cool Big Bands") . He was an arranger for Claude Thornhill and a collaborator with Miles Davis. In those sessions, he provided lush arrangements that his [Grove Music Online entry](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.09103) describes as characterized by the use of French horns and tubas and "restrained vibrato in the saxophones and brass" which "produced a rich, dark-textured, ‘cool’ orchestral sound."

    In the 1960s and 1970s, Gil Evans varied his approach. As the Grove Music Online article notes:

    From the early 1960s Evans made several attempts to form permanent orchestras, but these were unable to establish themselves, although they occasionally produced... excellent recordings... Later Evans incorporated electrified instruments (piano, bass guitar, synthesizer, etc.) into his ensembles, and tended to leave more space for solo improvisation in his arrangements and compositions. This led to a considerable loosening of his style in both form and texture compared with the more compact structures and veiled sonorities of his earlier arrangements.

    As Jeff Sultanof describes it, “In 1960, Gil Evans led an ensemble that was resident at the Jazz Gallery in New York, where he began a new phase of his music approach – letting the musicians develop the music on the stand during the gig.” [(Sultanof, p.139)](bibliography.html#sultanof-2017)

    Before legendary rock guitarist [Jimi Hendrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimi_Hendrix) died in 1970, he had been scheduled to collaborate with both Miles Davis and Gil Evans. In fact both Davis and Evans cite Hendrix as part of their inspiration to begin using electronic instruments in their ensembles. In an [interview for Jerry Jazz Musician](http://jerryjazzmusician.com/2001/12/stephanie-stein-crease-author-of-gil-evans-out-of-the-cool/) , Evans biographer Stephanie Stein said:

    Gil was very much interested in working with Jimi, and then Jimi died. A few years later, Gil resurrected the idea. Gil was one of the founding members of the New York Repertory Jazz Orchestra and for his first concert, in June 1973, he decided to do a Hendrix tribute, and he and several band members wrote arrangements of Hendrix tunes. A few days later, his band recorded that music for RCA. There is now a new reissue of that album, they did a fantastic job on the sound. And, from then on, the Hendrix charts stayed in Gil’s repertoire. He always included one or two Hendrix songs in a set, and they took on a much more “Evansian” quality.

    In many respects, Gil Evans's 1970s ensemble represented jazz/rock fusion in a big band setting.

    The Grove Music Online entries for both [Thad Jones](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:5982/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-2000234300?result=1&rskey=PyYR65#omo-9781561592630-e-2000234300-div1-2000234500) and [Mel Lewis](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:5982/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-1002242105) contain sections about the big band they co-led together. As Ted Gioia summarizes:

    from mid-1960s to the late 1970s, the Thad Jones-Mel Lewis band stood out as the most celebrated and polished of the New York big bands. Started in late 1965 as a rehearsal group, the band secured a Monday night gig at the Village Vanguard the following February. ([Gioia, *The History of Jazz* , 249](https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_History_of_Jazz/J9G50L3c14QC?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22band%20stood%20out%20as%20the%20most%20celebrated%20and%20polished%20of%20the%20new%20york%22))

    Thad Jones is from a musical family; you may have heard of his brothers Hank (piano) and Elvin (drums). Thad was a member of Count Basie's band for a while. We listened to him in this context twice in this course already, on " [Blues in Hoss's Flat](%24WIKI_REFERENCE%24/pages/1-dot-5-swing-big-bands "1.5 - Swing Big Bands") " and " [Everyday I Have the Blues](%24WIKI_REFERENCE%24/pages/3-dot-8-even-more-vocalists "3.8 - Even More Vocalists") ". Basie had commissioned Jones to write several new charts for a proposed album: *Count Basie Plays Thad Jones* . But, as Jeff Sultanof reports:

    "Jones's music was too advanced for the Basie band. Count cancelled the album but allowed Jones to keep the music.” [(Sultanof, p.131)](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Experiencing_Big_Band_Jazz/U7I3DwAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22Jones%E2%80%99s%20music%20was%20too%20advanced%20for%20the%20Basie%20band.%22)

    At least in part so that he could play these arrangements, Jones, with Lewis, formed a **rehearsal band** that was “driven by an interest among New York’s finest studio-recording and theater musicians to play more challenging music than they typically encountered in the recording and TV studios.” [(Lawn, p.356)](bibliography.html#lawn-2013) . They already had an outstanding set of arrangements that came out of the hard-swinging, bluesy Basie tradition, but which were somewhat more modern (rhythmically and harmonically complex). As Jones's Grove Music Online article reports:

    [Jones's] compositions provided substantial, flexible opportunities for the band's excellent soloists, and have become a staple in the repertory of school stage bands... They cover a wide range of styles, exploring waltz rhythms ( *The waltz you swang for me* , 1968 ), boogaloo and ballad combinations ( *Central Park North* , 1969 ), swing with traditional riffs or unpredictable melodies ( *The Big Dipper* , 1969 ; *Tiptoe* , 1970 ), bossa nova ( *It only happens every time* , 1970 ), bop ( *Fingers* , 1970 ), and jazz-rock ( *Greetings and Salutations* , 1975–6 ).

    We have also heard from Mel Lewis already in this course. He played with Stan Kenton on " [Recuerdos](%24WIKI_REFERENCE%24/pages/2-dot-5-cool-big-bands "2.5 - Cool Big Bands") ," Mel Tormé on " [Lullaby of Birdland](%24WIKI_REFERENCE%24/pages/2-dot-8-vocalists-of-the-40s-and-50s "2.8 - Vocalists of the 40s and 50s") ," and Gerald Wilson on " [Viva Tirado](%24WIKI_REFERENCE%24/pages/4-dot-2-big-bands-of-the-1960s "4.2 - Big Bands of the 1960s") ." If you're interested in a deep dive, UNT published an e-book about Mel Lewis that you can read for free online [here](https://iii.library.unt.edu/record=b5036060~S2) .

    The Thad Jones-Mel Lewis band began playing every Monday night at the Village Vanguard in February of 1966 - and, in a sense, they still do! After Jones left the band, it was renamed The Mel Lewis Orchestra. After Lewis died in 1990, the band renamed themselves the [Vanguard Jazz Orchestra](https://www.vanguardjazzorchestra.com/) . In this form, the band continues to perform every Monday.

- heading: Toshiko Akiyoshi & Lew Tabackin Big Band
  artists:
  - name: Toshiko Akiyoshi
    born: 1929
  - name: Lew Tabackin
    born: 1940
  prose: |-
    For brief biographical information, read the Grove Music Online articles about [Toshiko Akiyoshi](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.41268) and [Lew Tabackin](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J440200) . About their co-led big band of the 1970, once again, Ted Gioia provides a good introduction:

    The only major challenge to the Thad Jones-Mel Lewis Orchestra's preeminence as the leading mainstream large ensemble during the 1970s came from another city band with two leaders: the Toshiko Akiyoshi-Lew Tabackin Big Band, which flourished on the West Coast from 1973 to 1982. Akiyoshi was an unusual presence in the jazz world: an Asian woman, born in China and reared in Japan, who made her reputation in a field previously dominated by American males. A skilled pianist in a bud Powell vein, Akiyoshi wrote almost the entire book for the band, often drawing on her Japanese heritage in the same manner that Scott Joplin and Duke Ellington extracted art music from their African American roots. ([Gioia, *The History of Jazz* , 249](https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_History_of_Jazz/J9G50L3c14QC?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=%22The%20only%20major%20challenge%20to%20the%20Thad%20Jones-Mel%20Lewis%20Orchestra%27s%20preeminence%20as%20the%20leading%20mainstream%20large%20ensemble%20during%20the%201970s%22))

    Akiyoshi and Tabackin had just gotten married when they formed their band in 1970. In most of the books sourced for this class, their band is often mentioned alongside - indeed pitted against - the Thad Jones-Mel Lewis group. In a 1978 *Downbeat* readers’ poll, Akiyoshi-Tabackin won best big band by “a negligible number of votes" [(Lyons, p.308)](bibliography.html#lyons-1980) , with the Jones-Lewis group coming in a close second. In that same year's *Downbeat* Critics' Poll, the Jones-Lewis group won. In any case, the Akiyoshi-Tabackin Big Band is remembered as one of the most important and original big bands of the 1970s. Len Lyons, writing in 1980, opines:

    In an age when the big band is an endangered species... the Akiyoshi-Tabackin band is perhaps the last great hope for its continuance. The group... quickly acquired its identity through Toshiko's distinctive writing and her husband Lew Tabackin's soloing on flute and tenor sax... Their first album, *Kogun* ... became [Japan's] best-selling jazz album of all time. Its title track marked Akiyoshi's first successful synthesis of traditional Japanese sounds and modern jazz, an endeavor which reaches its artistic peak on "Minamata." This twenty-one-minute suite... is her crowning achievement as a composer. [(Lyons, p.306)](bibliography.html#lyons-1980)

    Akiyoshi is a talented pianist who played in the Bud Powell tradition. As an example, listen to her trio recording " [Pea, Bee and Lee](https://open.spotify.com/track/7wXDNoujO19uWAfLyw2V8n?si=g-ieDw3kQbOnAmLGe37JuA) ." Oscar Peterson heard her play in Japan and encouraged her to move to the US, which she did in 1956. She studied at Berklee College of Music, where she was the first-ever Asian student.

    You can watch an interview with Akiyoshi (in 10 short parts) on the [Berklee website](https://archives.berklee.edu/toshiko-akiyoshi/bca-011-toshiko-akiyoshi) . Listen at least to [part 8 of that interview](https://archives.berklee.edu/bohp2007-09-29takiyoshi-chapter-8/19445) , in which she discusses incorporating Japanese music into her jazz compositions. She mentions that members of her family were involved in traditional Japanese performance arts: her sister was a "Japanese traditional dancer" and her father "was a student of Noh, and he [got to a] point where he was so much into it [that] he was studying [Ōtsuzumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Ctsuzumi) also." [Noh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noh) is a traditional form of Japanese theater associated with a particular kind of music that involves drums and a unique way of singing, both of which feature prominently during some sections of "Minamata."

    In a [2009 review for a compilation album](https://www.allaboutjazz.com/toshiko-akiyoshi-lew-tabackin-big-band-mosaic-select-by-samuel-chell.php) , author Samuel Chell offered the following description of the group:

    The music is as exhilarating to hear as it is challenging to play. Close your eyes and imagine what the immortal pianist Bud Powell would sound like were his best compositions and solos orchestrated by Duke Ellington, fortified by the virile-sounding tenor saxophone of Sonny Rollins as primary soloist, embellished with some exotic 5/4 and 13/8 meters, flavored with some traditional Japanese melodies, and finally entrusted to a group of musicians committed to a man to keep swing king at all costs—and you may have some sense, albeit an imperfect one, of the distinctive sound of the Akiyoshi-Tabackin big band of the 1970s.
