  This lesson covers several revered musicians who built solo careers during the 1970s and 1980s. Many of these musicians recorded for **[ECM Records](https://www.ecmrecords.com)** , a German record company that became associated with a particular sound and approach to jazz music. Drummers especially may know what I mean. "ECM" has come to roughly describe a kind of straight-eight groove that draws from bossa nova, boogaloo, and rock rhythms. Here's a YouTube video I found of a drummer explaining how he thinks of the "ECM groove":

  <iframe referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/Txe--PwJhEY">
  </iframe>

  So many important musicians recorded with ECM. Among them are Paul Bley, Jan Garbarek, Gary Burton, Chick Corea, Jack DeJohnette, Keith Jarrett, Pat Metheny, Dave Liebman, and John Abercrombie. More recently, Mark Turner, Ethan Iverson, Vijay Iyer, Avishai Cohen (tp), and Chris Potter have all recorded with ECM. If you're curious, a more complete list of ECM records can be found on [this Wikipedia page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ECM_Records_albums) .

  But before we get to the main artists of this lesson, first a detour. Jazz-rock fusion music heralded the development of several musical trends that merged jazz with popular/contemporary styles. Herbie Hancock produced produced both [disco](https://open.spotify.com/track/6hfTNwjIIbhqCeke0qmTJp?si=Qta5nmfhRg2JgwtrVeDc-Q) and ["DJ scratch" R&B](https://open.spotify.com/track/2nWHzbBWBOePUvAImQv2So?si=xriKnFEyT_unJpwQt1tVsg) .  Miles Davis recorded covers of [pop music](https://open.spotify.com/track/2WovWnvG8WtHie7RnHGKrk?si=t7Gg-O5gT5S2K5SkZINaMQ) . And artists like [Grover Washington](https://open.spotify.com/album/50gtvo0hufJj06ipbOjAiU?si=vJWPC_UlRjyZ0je-Otc3Lg) and [Grant Green](https://open.spotify.com/track/5RrtSMRbKKT2JjhHo6adH5?si=Ed9Nc-n9TIKvTAWhHUQGQg) recorded backbeat-driven instrumental music featuring improvisation over jazz chords. In the 1980s and 1990s, one such trend was quite commercially successful: that which we today call **smooth jazz** .

  Check out the [Grove Music Online article about smooth jazz](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2257316) . It highlights some important characteristics of the genre:

  ...the use of electronic instruments, heavy emphasis on “backbeat” rhythms rather than swing, the use of popular repertoire, simple harmonic progressions, and short improvisations... Many of these compositions featured uncomplicated harmonic progressions—sometimes with as few as one or two chords. Although some traditionalists derided the lack of harmonic complexity, many crossover artists viewed these progressions as an opportunity to focus more on groove, melody, and other musical elements.

  In 1986, saxophonist Kenneth Bruce Gorelick - best known as Kenny G - released his album *Duotones,* featuring the hit single " [Songbird](https://youtu.be/QN2RnjFHmNY) ." That album went on to sell more than five million copies in the United States alone. It is, to date, the biggest-selling instrumental record all time. Kenny G holds the record as the best-selling instrumental musician of the modern era (excluding Beethoven and the like), with global sales totaling more than 75 million records.

  Smooth jazz is frequently criticized, though less often by listeners than by jazz musicians themselves. Guitarist Pat Metheny was [notoriously critical of Kenny G](http://www.jazzoasis.com/methenyonkennyg.htm) . And here's how [that Guitar Channel article about Mahavishnu Orchestra](https://www.guitar-channel.com/rich_murray/mahavishnu-orchestra-40th-anniversary.html) derisively describes the birth of smooth jazz from fusion:

  For all of its exciting promise, with few exceptions, the jazz-rock movement all but fizzled out several years later when greedy recording executives predicted a larger market for a simplified watered down version of the music. In what may be one of the most ironic outcomes in music history, dumbed-down jazz-rock became the unwitting stepfather of smooth jazz. There were a few brave musicians who fought against The Wave, but they were commercially drowned out. In a business world that creates and then worships its own demographic models and sees art as only a numbers game, innovation and virtuosity didn't stand a chance.

  Smooth jazz is still discussed today more as a temporary spin-off of "proper" jazz. I haven't seen it mentioned in more than a few history books, and then only briefly. On the strength of its commercial success, I think you should know about it. But it doesn't appear on our class playlist.

  ## Vocabulary
  ECM Records - German record company founded in 1969. Recognized for its excellent recordings of free jazz, European jazz artists, and prominent artists from the 1970s. ECM has a readily identifiable house style, uniting two previously disparate genres, jazz-rock and free jazz. ([Grove Music Online](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.48928))

  Smooth Jazz - A label used for an American radio format that emerged in the late 1980s that also has come to designate a substyle of jazz that features rock beats, medium-slow tempos, electronic instruments, popular repertory, and (compared to other forms of jazz) simple harmonies and melodies.

    Read the [Grove Music Online article about Jarrett](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J223100) . He was a prodigy classical pianist as a child. He came to prominence as a member of Charles Lloyd's group (their first album together was [*Dream Weaver*](https://open.spotify.com/album/3lAHM9enj2ctgKZ1hFnA5i?si=vL8HKmwlShOPpCCY9AD0JQ) ). He went on to play with Blakey's Jazz Messengers, and then Miles Davis (replacing Chick Corea). The Grove article notes Jarrett's "flawless technique, intense lyricism, and total physical involvement with the piano." It continues:

    In 1972 Jarrett began performing solo concerts which consisted simply of two extended improvisations, each usually 30 to 45 minutes in length. The music spanned a rich variety of traditions, but was developed in a manner that seemed holistic rather than merely eclectic, illuminating Jarrett's reference to his work as universal folk music. Through the international success of these concerts he became the only jazz artist of the 1970s to capture a mass audience without conforming to commercial trends. Furthermore he spearheaded a revival of interest in acoustic music, as he refused to play electronic instruments after leaving Davis's band.

    Jarrett's *Köln Concert* was one such entirely improvised concert. The album features four tracks, encompassing two long sets identified as Part I and Part II. The last track, Part II C, was an encore.

    Listen to the first 5 minutes of [this episode of the Ted Radio Hour](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/719557642) , which discusses the circumstances of the *Köln* ([Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne)) Concert. It's a striking story. By mistake, the wrong piano was on stage, and there was no time to correct it. Jarrett was on the verge of refusing to play, and yet the concert that resulted produced the best selling solo piano album - also the best selling solo jazz album - of all time!

  artists:
  - name: Pat Metheny
    born: 1954
  prose: |-
    Read about Patrick Bruce Metheny in [his Grove Music Online article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.41302) , which identifies him as a jazz fusion guitarist. He picked up the guitar at 14 and only 6 years later was teaching guitar at Berklee College of Music. While there, he played in the band of vibraphonist Gary Burton (see below). Later, he formed his own group that featured keyboardist and UNT alum Lyle Mays (1953-2020). Metheny has won at least 20 Grammy awards, and still records and tours actively today.

    The Grove article discusses Metheny's style and influence:

    Metheny is the central figure in a transformation of the basic instrumentation of jazz from the mid-1970s onwards, in which the guitar has attained a stature in jazz equal to that of the tenor saxophone. In 1977 he created a new and now widely imitated sound, in which a warm, smooth, full-bodied guitar timbre is modified via a quivering digital delay produced by overlapping signals projected through different speakers. The character of the sound contrasts dramatically with both the nearly monochromatic timbres which have dominated jazz guitar from the days of Charlie Christian onward, and the diverse ostentatious and aggressive timbres which John McLaughlin and others brought to jazz-rock.

    Metheny's first album as a leader was recorded in 1975 (at age 21!) with drummer Bob Moses and bassist Jaco Pastorius, who we heard already playing with the fusion band Weather Report. Pastorius deserves some special attention here. He was - arguably still is - the most influential electric bass player in jazz. He played a fretless electric bass, which he created himself by removing the frets from his bass and filling in the grooves with epoxy resin. The fretless electric bass is a relatively uncommon instrument even today. It requires even more precise intonation than a fretted electric bass does. That makes Pastorius's virtuosity all the more extraordinary. [His Grove Music Online article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J347400) has this to say about his style:

    Unlike many jazz and rock bass guitarists, Pastorius used a fretless instrument, and played with immaculate intonation and melodic clarity ([*Donna Lee*](https://open.spotify.com/track/7CfCkdTNWFQDopWol8SWYQ?si=hZJwR758T2mf2ext8iHRtg)). Although sometimes faulted for his flamboyant stage personality and eclecticism, he won the admiration of jazz and rock bass players for his fleet technique, incorporating among other features an unprecedented facility for producing artificial harmonics on the instrument ([*Portrait of Tracy*](https://open.spotify.com/track/2Fc3GG0g44NbggiPUKM7AG?si=HoTnX3HTSaSeGfHt_js4zA)) and the imaginative fusion of styles in his solos.

    ### Michael Brecker (1949-2007)

    Read about Michael Brecker on [Grove Music Online](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J056700) . His early career involved as much rock as jazz, and he developed a hybrid style that was especially well-suited to fusion of the 70s-90s. As the article explains:

    Brecker's style has been, after that of Coltrane, one of the strongest influences on young jazz tenor saxophonists.

    *Some Skunk Funk* , on the album [*The Brecker Brothers*](https://open.spotify.com/album/3mXY4gBVlEzTk4DVGhFTpZ?si=UmtAABosTO2FGx8U41GYQA) , epitomizes Michael's preference for virtuoso, starkly angular, chromatically tinged melody inserted into an aggressive, syncopated, jazz-funk setting; a later album title described the style as [*Heavy Metal Bebop*](https://open.spotify.com/album/5Ogp1XkplgR6hO099uEjIL?si=-kykQE8VS5mi4jL_yYqUJg) .

    In the 1970s and 80, Michael Brecker contributed to more than 400 pop albums. He has recorded with Steely Dan, Joni Mitchell, Eric Clapton, Billy Joel, John Lennon, Aerosmith, Frank Sinatra, Frank Zappa, Bruce Springsteen, Parliament-Funkadelic, and Paul Simon. He toured twice with Herbie Hancock's quartet.

    He and his brother Randy co-ran a venue in New York called Seventh Avenue South. Jam sessions there led to the creation of the fusion band Steps Ahead (first called "Steps"). We heard Michael Brecker already playing with that group on "Pools" in our [fusion lesson](./fusion.html) .

    ### Randy Brecker (1945-)

    Here's [Randy Brecker's Grove Music Online Article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J056800) . He studied at Indiana University. He was a founding member of [Blood, Sweat, and Tears.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood,_Sweat_%26_Tears) Then he played with Horace Silver and a number of big bands, including the Thad Jones and Mel Lewis group and Jaco Pastorius's Word of Mouth band. Like his brother Michael, Randy was an in-demand studio musician. The Grove article describes him as:

    ...a versatile musician who has played bop, rock, and jazz-rock; he has expressed a preference for [jazz-rock] because it offers him the greatest scope for inventive composition and performance.

    He can [still be heard regularly](http://randybrecker.com/itinerary/) at jazz festivals and venues around the world. He was in Denton as recently as 2018 for the Denton Arts and Jazz Fest.

    Read the [Grove Music Online article about Wynton Marsalis](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.6002278226) . It notes a few key ideas, some of which we will explore again in Unit 5 when we discuss Jazz at Lincoln Center. For now, note the following key points about his career.

    He's from a musical New Orleans family. His father is pianist Ellis Marsalis. His brothers are Branford (ts), who also plays on the track below, Delfeayo (tb) after whom the track is named, and Jason (d).

    Wynton studied at Julliard and, while there, played with Jazz Messengers. He played briefly with Herbie Hancock as part of [V.S.O.P.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartet_(Herbie_Hancock_album)) , taking over from Freddie Hubbard. In 1982, he formed a quintet with his brother Branford. The 1960s post bop-inspired sound of this group drew attention at a time when the dominant sound in jazz was fusion, and some lamented that traditional jazz was going away. The members of this quintet were sometimes referred to as the "young lions."

    The Grove article notes:

    Marsalis is an extraordinarily gifted trumpeter, and his first albums made an enormous impression: here was someone with dazzling technical perfection and a feeling for jazz, though not an innovator. He began his jazz career under the heavy influence of Clifford Brown and Freddie Hubbard. During the latter half of the 1980s he set aside his activities in classical and baroque playing to focus on emulating the mid-1960s trumpet style of Miles Davis and then to investigate swing, in which setting he showed a special interest in the growl-and-plunger techniques developed by Bubber Miley and others of Ellington's orchestra. He is also fluent in traditional jazz...

    In both 1983 and 1984, Wynton won Grammy awards in both classical and jazz categories. In 1983 the awards were for [*Trumpet Concertos: Haydn, Hummel, Mozart*](https://open.spotify.com/album/27MceTtP8V5SYgDIVfUExa?si=HPqa6h70QtS2wJ-bIvZeyQ) and [*Think of One*](https://open.spotify.com/album/52nzub1zJXZ9htSr9Zcbxc?si=6SiyyDv2RySNyPAm6Y_9LA) . In 1985 the awards were for [*Baroque Music for Trumpet*](https://open.spotify.com/album/57Wy40o6Ks5u3J6uFYpQqb?si=5ISSDRURRu6Dz9rqWQRo0g) and [*Hot House Flowers*](https://open.spotify.com/album/1k5A9i3hIBHm3XKpWBFeNX?si=LLZBoF8dS6yVzrSwZD_ahA) . He remains the only artist to have ever won both classical and jazz Grammy awards in the same year.

    Gary Burton is one of the most influential vibraphone players. He attended Berklee from 1960 to 1961. Since 1971, he's also been faculty there, and as such has been an important educator for much of his career. Read the [Grove Music Online article about him](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:5982/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-2000068600) , which notes:

    A virtuoso vibraphonist, Burton developed an original style of improvisation quite distinct from those of his influential predecessors on the instrument, Lionel Hampton and Milt Jackson. In the early 1960s he pioneered a now widely copied playing style that made use of four mallets at once... In many ways Burton created a compromise between contemporary jazz piano and wind styles... One of the few modern jazz improvisers not to have drawn substantially on the melodic conceptions of the bop pioneers Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie, he replaced their vocabulary with a fresh one that emphasizes 20th-century classical music as well as country music. He frequently employs accompanying devices rich in vamps and pedal points, reminiscent of country music, as well as the flavor of Latin American styles.
