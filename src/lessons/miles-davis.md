heading: Miles Dewey Davis III (1926-1991)
intro: |-
  We have encountered Miles Davis in previous lessons with Charlie Parker and Gil Evans. Davis is celebrated for his stylistic diversity—he was an innovator in bebop, hard bop, cool, modal, third stream, and fusion. He was also an exceptional bandleader who curated ensembles that were often greater than the sum of their parts.

  Known for his minimalistic and lyrical playing style, Davis admired Ahmad Jamal’s economy of notes and encouraged his pianists to emulate that approach. He attended Juilliard but considered the bebop scene his real education. He favored middle and low registers and often employed a Harmon mute. Unlike many bebop players, he avoided common clichés and focused on melodic invention.

  prose: |-
    After his bebop and cool phases, Davis led what is now known as the “First Great Quintet” in the mid-1950s, featuring John Coltrane, Red Garland, Paul Chambers, and Philly Joe Jones. This group combined the fire of bebop with the soulful, bluesy feeling of hard bop. In 1956, to fulfill contractual obligations to Prestige, they recorded four iconic albums in just two sessions: *Workin’, Cookin’, Relaxin’,* and *Steamin’* .

- heading: Modal Era
  prose: |-

- heading: Second Great Quintet (Post-Bop)
  prose: |-
    The “Second Great Quintet” (Davis, Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, Tony Williams) pushed jazz into post-bop territory. Harmony was freer, chords less functional, and the rhythm section more interactive and open. Hired at 17, Tony Williams brought a fresh rhythmic conception. The group’s approach was spontaneous and exploratory.

- heading: Fusion Era
  prose: |-
    In the late 1960s, Davis pioneered jazz-rock fusion, mixing electric instruments, rock rhythms, and avant-garde harmonies. *Bitches Brew* (1970) is a landmark, featuring extensive improvisation, minimal instructions, and heavy studio editing. The personnel on *Bitches Brew* included future stars of 1970s fusion.

  prose: |-
