intro: |-
  Perhaps drummers led the charge into hard bop. The role of drummers in hard bop contrasts greatly with that of cool jazz. Hard bop drummers (e.g., Blakey, Roach, Philly Joe Jones) “accompanied, but also commented on and completed” soloists’ statements. Sometimes they were even criticized as “too loud, or even crude.” [(Porter, p.257)](bibliography.html#porter-ullman-1993)

  - Bebop sometimes seemed pretentious. Hard Bop was bluesy and accessible.
  - Hard Bop was generally slower than bebop, though not as languid and relaxed as “cool” jazz.
  - Hard bop groups preferred tight arrangements; this wasn’t just off-the-cuff jam session material.
  - There was a greater emphasis on original music. Hard bop compositions were less commonly contrafacts.
  - Drummers rarely used brushes.

- heading: Sonny Rollins
  artists:
  - name: Sonny Rollins
    born: 1930
  prose: |-
    Walter Theodore Rollins was given his nickname "Sonny" by Clifford Brown. Miles Davis gave Rollins the nickname "Newk" because of his resemblance to baseball player Don Newcombe.

    Rollins replaced Harold Land in the Brown-Roach group. Note that personnel below are the same as for “Daahoud,” except Rollins replaces Land. This is the Brown-Roach group, but on a Sonny Rollins release.

    Gunther Schuller wrote an often-cited essay about Rollins’s improvisation, calling attention to his “skill in constructing solos through the manipulation of simple musical motives.” This may have intimidated Rollins, who remarked, “When I read that I was sort of taken aback, because I didn’t know what I was doing.” [(Gioia, p.283)](bibliography.html#gioia-2011) And “I really didn’t understand what I was doing until I read Gunther Schuller… This thing about the thematic approach. I guess it’s true, but I had never thought about it; I was just playing it.” [(Lyons, p.204)](bibliography.html#lyons-1980) .

    Benjamin Givan disagrees with Schuller's analysis in the following article, which is worth reading if you want a deeper dive into the jazz performance practice of Rollins and his contemporaries:
    <https://academics.skidmore.edu/blogs/bgivan/files/2014/07/GIVAN-Schuller-Rollins-JAMS.pdf>

    There's an [effort to rename the Williamsburg bridge in NYC after Rollins](http://sonnyrollinsbridge.net/) , since Rollins famously took a short sabbatical from recording, during which time he could be heard practicing there.

- heading: Art Blakey
  artists:
  - name: Art Blakey
    born: 1919
    died: 1990
  prose: |-
    “He’s exceptionally accurate as well as powerful. Blakey’s trademark as a drummer may be his forceful, quartz-steady closing of the hi-hat on every second and fourth beat, which became standard practice during the fifties. This beat is accompanied by strong attacks on the ride cymbal, and by frequent loud snare and bass drum accents in triplets or other cross-rhythms. He might chatter on the rims of a tom-tom for color, stutter in triplets on his snare, or dig his elbow into a tom-tom to change its pitch. He uses drum rolls that seem to explode. He rarely goes in for merely subtle effects: when he works over the tom-toms he sounds ready to swing a marching band.” [(Porter, p.268)](bibliography.html#porter-ullman-1993)

    Blakey originally played piano, but when Erroll Garner joined his band, Blakey switched to drums.

    He formed the “Seventeen Messengers” in 1947, then later pared the group down to a quintet. He formed the quintet in 1955. It would last for thirty years (!) and become known for cultivating future stars. It only disbanded with Blakey’s death in 1990.

    Famous alums of the Jazz Messengers include: Jackie McLean, Kenny Garrett, Sonny Stitt, Wayne Shorter, Wynton and Branford Marsalis, Freddie Hubbard, Woody Shaw, Horace Silver, Cedar Walton, Keith Jarrett, Bennie Green, Mulgrew Miller, Peter Washington.

    This group recorded the 1956 album *Hard Bop* , for which the genre would be named.

    The group's early recordings stayed close to Brown-Roach model of hard bop, but 1955’s “The Preacher” marked a turn toward a funkier, more blues and gospel-influenced style.

    For a while, Blakey practiced Islam and changed his name to Abdullah Ibn Buhaina.
