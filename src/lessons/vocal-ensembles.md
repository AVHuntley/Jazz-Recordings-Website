heading: Introduction
intro: |-
  In this lesson, we're going to look at jazz singing in a group context. Just like in other large ensembles, when several vocalists sing together, there's somewhat less room for improvisation. Parts need to be carefully arranged to work together, and the emphasis is often on the overall effect of the piece rather than an individual's performance.

  An important concept for this lesson is **vocalese**
  - the practice of writing and singing lyrics to preexisting melodies. These melodies can be the composed "head" of a song, or, frequently, a recorded improvised solo. The [Grove Music Online article about vocalese](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.50592) credits Eddie Jefferson with pioneering the style in the 1940s and King Pleasure with popularizing it in the 1950s. As an example, listen to " [Moody's Mood for Love,](https://open.spotify.com/track/68t4fZTs5ajxuL5M1OndmM?si=dg8LNATRQcSTgfwjXtPqWw) " performed by King Pleasure. The melody comes from an [improvised solo by James Moody](https://open.spotify.com/track/1hE1mXULzuXL80kfWCWOne?si=jgeRqxSqT0ev29nb5MxTeg) over the standard "I'm in the Mood for Love." Then Eddie Jefferson wrote lyrics that fit the improvised solo, and King Pleasure recorded them.

  Group singing dates back to prehistory. The voice, after all, is the oldest instrument in human history. But the first group singing style that often cited as influencing jazz is **barbershop quartet singing** . Read the [Grove Music Online article about it](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2218601) . The style has an interesting history, first emerging in the late 1800s as an African American style but formally codified in the 1940s:

  The origins of barbershop singing have yet to be located precisely. The style likely emerged amid a thriving tradition of African American recreational quartet singing in the 1880s that drew on commercial influences from blackface minstrelsy, various professional touring quartets, ragtime, and the sheet music industry. Professional touring quartets, variety shows, early recordings, and sheet music publications popularized the style.

  A series of nostalgic revivals in the late 1920s and 1930s led to the formal definition of the style, creation of the barbershop canon, and the founding of Barbershop Harmony Society... in 1939 . This revival movement also constructed a narrative of barbershop’s origins as amateur, rural, and primarily white, thus obscuring the importance of African American groups and musical practices...

  These days, you're more likely to hear [barbershop singing at Disneyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dapper_Dans) than in a jazz club. Nevertheless, it was an important early influence on jazz singers.

  UNT, of course, has a tradition of vocal ensembles in the form of the [Jazz Singers, Avenue C, Third Street, and West End](https://jazz.unt.edu/untjazzsingers) .

  ## Vocabulary
  **Vocalese**
  - jazz singing wherein words are sung note for note to melodies that were originally created by a soloist's improvisation. Distinct from scat singing in that words are used instead of nonsense syllables. Vocalese lyrics are often about jazz and jazz musicians.

  **Barbershop quartet singing** : “Characterized by homophonic, close-harmony, four-part arrangements… with the melody performed by the second tenor, or “lead.” The style frequently uses "snakes" or "swipes," which is when a chord is altered by a change in one or more non-melodic voices. It was popular 1810-1910, but was re-popularized in the 1940s, when the style we hear today was codified. ([Grove Music Online](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2218601))

- heading: The Four Freshmen
  prose: |-
    The Four Freshmen is a vocal group formed in 1948 by Don Barbour (1927-), Ross Barbour (1928-2011), Bob Flanigan (1926-2011), and Hal Kratzsch (1925-1970). Their vocal arrangements influenced every other group in this lesson. They also frequently accompanied themselves with instruments. [Their Grove Music Online article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.47705) states:

    The group represented a modernizing force in the sphere of close harmony quartets in American popular music, moving away from the barbershop style to introduce elements of jazz. In doing so, they influenced younger groups such as the Hi-Los and the Beach Boys.

    The group has gone through several personnel changes over the years, but [still survives today](https://www.fourfreshmen.com) .

- heading: Manhattan Transfer & Bobby McFerrin
  artists:
  - name: Bobby McFerrin

  prose: |-
    **Manhattan Transfer** was formed in 1969 by Tim Hauser (1941-2014). Like the Four Freshmen, this group has undergone personnel changes over the years, but the brand still survives and performs today. You can read about the group's history on [their website](https://manhattantransfer.net/about/) . Their [Grove Music Online article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.47716) notes that the group was:

    intended to revive and update the skilful close-harmony vocalizations associated with such groups as the Four Freshmen and Lambert, Hendricks and Ross.

    **Robert Keith "Bobby" McFerrin Jr. (1950-)** is a virtuoso vocalist whose career took off in the 1980s. Though he does sing with groups, among his most original artistic feats are his solo performances. He has an incredibly wide vocal range and can leap with precision between his uppermost and lowermost registers, accompanying himself by singing bass lines between the notes of the melody. He also at times taps his chest with his hand, providing a kind of percussion effect.

    You've probably heard his song "Don't Worry, Be Happy." It was a huge commercial success when it was released in 1989. To date, McFerrin has won (at least) 10 Grammy awards.

    [His Grove Music Online article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J280300) provides an excellent description of his musical style:

    In his early performances and recordings McFerrin usually sang with accompaniment, and his style at this period drew heavily on Lambert, Hendricks, and Ross. But during the mid-1980s he took a new direction, singing mostly improvised lines that explore timbre, attack, and resonance within a framework of established harmonies, meters, and repetitive rhythmic patterns; he uses elements of a wide variety of vocal styles, sometimes encompassing several within a single song. In unaccompanied songs he performs, consecutively, several parts of a multipartite texture (percussion, bass, solo voice, and sometimes inner voices), exploiting all kinds of vocal sound from popping noises and grunts to swooping falsetto. He has an astonishing range (usually from *D* to *f* ″, but occasionally up to *f* ‴) and accuracy of pitch, and a solid but fluid, agile control in all registers. His deep conviction that everyone has the potential to make music leads him to involve audiences in his performances, asking them to maintain ostinato choral parts against which he sings a lively counterpoint.

    For an example of McFerrin involving his audience, I recommended watching [this video of his performance of Bach's WTC Prelude #1 in C Major](https://youtu.be/PgvJg7D6Qck)

  - name: Jon Hendricks
    born: 1921
    died: 2017
  prose: |-
    Jon Hendricks was a vocalist, lyricist, and one of the most influential practitioners of vocalese. He has been called by some the “The Poet Laureate of Jazz." Read [his Grove Music Online article](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.J198700) ; it provides brief insight into a fascinating history involving military service in WWII, childhood collaborations with Art Tatum, and his many professional collaborations. It describes his style like this:

    Hendricks excelled both at setting lyrics to complex melodies and at performing those lyrics in an articulate manner, with the finesse of a virtuoso saxophonist. He was a fine scat singer, and was so adept at imitating instrumental sounds that his improvisations often surpassed the solos played by his accompanists.

    Hendricks died in 2017. Just a couple years prior, he participated in an interview with Michael Mwenso for Jazz at Lincoln Center. Check out the first episode of that six-part series here:

- heading: New York Voices
  prose: |-
    New York Voices is a vocal group founded in 1987 by Peter Eldridge, Caprice Fox, Sara Krieger, Darmon Meader, and Kim Nazarian, each an alum of Ithaca College. Like Manhattan Transfer and the Four Freshmen, this group still exists, albeit with slightly different personnel. You can read about the band and each member on [their official bio page](https://newyorkvoices.com/bios/) , which describes the origins of the band:

    **New York Voices** was born in the mid-1980s at upstate New York's Ithaca College. **Peter Eldridge** , an aspiring jazz pianist who came late to singing; **Kim Nazarian** , who dreamed of a life on Broadway; and **Darmon Meader** , a saxophonist and self-described “choral geek,” came together in the school's vocal jazz ensemble. Director Dave Reilly included all three of them, as well as **Caprice Fox** , in an ensemble he'd been invited to bring on a tour of European jazz festivals. “It seemed to go over so well that Darmon and I decided to keep it going, try to make something happen professionally,” Eldridge recalls. “Kim and Caprice were part of that original band, and we slowly but surely made the transition to New York City in 1988.” There they met **Sara Krieger** , who became the fifth member of the band Reilly had named “New York Voices.”
