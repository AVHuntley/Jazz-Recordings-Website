  Read the [Grove Music article on Soul Jazz](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.45698) .

  Soul jazz originates from hard bop, but is more heavily influenced by soul, R&B, Motown, and the blues. Like hard bop, soul jazz features a strong backbeat and blues and gospel-type chords. But compared to hard bop, soul jazz features simpler harmonies, shorter solos, and more clearly defined dance rhythms. Soul jazz groups often include Hammond B3 organ, tenor sax, and guitar.

  Listen for the different style of soloing in soul jazz compared to hard bop and bebop. There are some bebop elements, but there are more bluesy and lyrical melodic lines than there are in bebop. The musicians rarely try to play "out." They were trying to be accessible. In fact, soul jazz musicians often made recordings shorter on purpose (<4mins) so they’d be ready for radio play.

  We've already listened to a few recordings that could be called soul jazz: “Moanin’” by Art Blakey; “The Sidewinder” by Lee Morgan; and even “Song for My Father” by Horace Silver. (Don't think of "Song for My Father" as soul jazz? Check out [this 1966 James Brown cover](https://open.spotify.com/track/51dVte6AIJYZQKvvQqFmBQ?si=it5zlDmrTKOAnNBoH7JCcQ) .)

    “One of the most important electric guitarists in jazz since Charlie Christian and Django Reinhardt.” [(Bierman, p.251)](bibliography.html#bierman-2016)

    Read the [Grove Music article on Wes Montgomery](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:5982/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-0000041303) . Note the passages about his trademark techniques (emphasis added):

    Instead of a plectrum **he used his thumb** for various kinds of strumming, achieving a softer attack and leaving his fingers free to pluck octaves and chordal passages. His mastery of technique created a sensation among younger guitarists, and **the playing of octaves, in particular, became a trademark of the Montgomery style** .

    Montgomery tended to build his solos from melodies in single notes to octave passages and finally to **chords** .

    Montgomery is said to have pioneered three jazz guitar techniques:

    - Using his thumb instead of a pick
    - Soloing by playing chords (instead of just single notes)
    - Playing lines in octaves

    His legacy can be heard in many “smooth” jazz guitarists, like George Benson.

    For a slightly more in-depth discussion of the Hammond organ, check out [this article from the website Made In Chicago Museum](https://www.madeinchicagomuseum.com/single-post/hammond-organ-co) .

    Jimmy Smith studied the piano when he was young, “occasionally getting pointers from a young pianist… Bud Powell” [(Deveaux & Giddins, p.330)](bibliography.html#deveaux-giddins-2011) . However, in 1953, after hearing [Wild Bill Davis play the organ](https://open.spotify.com/album/1BFvbL3XN47sLZfZrMGPj1?si=6phpHCJVQ16aC2lNMdet0w) , Smith bought one.

    Smith is the common ancestor to all subsequent jazz organists. Few, if any, organ players today escape his influence. He practically defined what the jazz organ is supposed to sound like. Here are a few things to note about organ playing:

    - There are two keyboards (or " [manuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manual_(music)) "), and a set of foot pedals.
    - The organist can control the timbre of each manual separately by manipulating its many drawbars.
    - The organ is typically amplified by a rotating Leslie speaker, the rotational speed of which can be adjusted on the fly.
    - The classic organ trio consists of organ, drums, and guitar (no bass player).
    - The organist plays the bass lines both in their left hand and with the foot pedals.

    Her playing on the Hammond organ was known for its hard-driving attack and its distinctive mixture of soulful licks and angular bop melodies.

[(Bierman, p.250)](bibliography.html#bierman-2016) Unlike Jimmy Smith, however, Scott frequently worked with a bass player. That freed both of her hands to play chords. On many recordings, Scott plays the organ almost like a piano. Listen for big chords that require two hands to play.

    **Stanley Turrentine** is often remembered for his blues and soul approach to the tenor saxophone. He collaborated with both Jimmy Smith and Shirley Scott (to whom he was married during the 1960s). His output, especially later in his career, was often more pop than jazz. As usual when a jazz musician plays popular styles, this choice met some criticism from straight-ahead jazz players.

    Check out the Grove Music Online article about Turrentine. Also read [this article](https://www.npr.org/2008/09/10/94436220/stanley-turrentine-saxophone-sugar-man) , which accompanies an episode of NPR's *Jazz Profiles* series hosted by Nancy Wilson. If you have some time, also check out the associated radio story on that same page. Note the following points from the article:

    Turrentine married jazz organist Shirley Scott in 1960; during their partnership, they made some of their most heralded music together. When they moved to Philadelphia, they befriended Hammond B-3 organ pioneer [Jimmy Smith](https://www.npr.org/2008/09/10/templates/story/story.php?storyId=15404715) , and Turrentine fully immersed himself in the jazz organ sound. He even recorded on Smith's classic album *Midnight Special* .

    Turrentine's first album for CTI, 1970's *Sugar* , yielded the classic tune of the same name.... His relative success, despite his continued ability to deliver in the straight-ahead jazz vein, led to a predictable backlash among critics.

    Here's the [Grove Music Online article about Cannonball Adderley](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2226820) . Note the following:

    From late 1957 he worked with Miles Davis, during which time he performed on a number of Davis’s most important recordings, notably *Milestones, Porgy & Bess* and *Kind of Blue* .

    Adderley achieved his greatest success between 1959 and 1975 with a band that included his brother Nat. The group was one of the early proponents of soul jazz and had a number of hits, including Bobby Timmons’s “This Here” (aka “Dis Here”) Adderley’s “Sack O’ Woe”, and Joe Zawinul’s “Mercy, Mercy, Mercy”.

    Adderley’s saxophone playing was firmly rooted in blues, gospel, and the jazz styles of Coleman Hawkins, Benny Carter, and Charlie Parker. He was no great innovator, but instead built his reputation on consolidating and blending existing jazz styles in his own fashion.

    Cannonball worked closely with his brother [Nat(haniel)](https://libproxy.library.unt.edu:2147/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.A2226826) for many years. You can learn about them both from the [*NPR Profiles* article and radio episode about Nat](https://www.npr.org/2008/08/20/93749946/nat-adderley-brotherly-swing) .

    Joe Zawinul was an important pianist and keyboard player, especially in the 1970s when he led, with Wayne Shorter, the popular fusion band Weather Report. Zawinul was an important pioneer of the use of electronics and synthesizers in jazz music.
